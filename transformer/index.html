How a Transformer reads a sentence

A Transformer understands a word by looking at the other words around it. That looking is called attention, and it's the idea behind ChatGPT, Google Translate and almost every modern language model.

A hands-on companion to Jay Alammar's The Illustrated Transformer, which explains the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need.

The machine's layout

The article's example is translation. A French sentence goes in and an English one comes out. From far away, the Transformer is a black box. Open it and there are two halves.

The encoder reads the whole input sentence and builds an understanding of every word. The decoder writes the output one word at a time, checking back with the encoder as it goes. The paper stacks six encoders and six decoders. Each encoder layer has the same shape but its own weights, the numbers it learns during training.

One property matters a lot. Inside the encoder, every word flows along its own path. The paths only mix in self-attention; in the feed-forward network each word is processed separately. So all the words can be processed at the same time, in parallel. Older models such as RNNs read one word after another. That parallelism is why Transformers train so much faster.

2

Words become numbers

A model can only do math, so each word is first turned into a list of numbers called a vector. This step is the embedding. In the paper each word becomes 512 numbers. Below, each word is drawn as 16 colored cells, with darker meaning a bigger number.

There's a catch. Self-attention treats the sentence as a bag of words, with no sense of order. To fix that, the model adds a second vector to each word, a positional encoding, that says where the word sits.

What a positional encoding looks like

Each row is the position vector added to the word in that position (1 to 20). Each column is one of its numbers. The left half comes from sine waves and the right half from cosine waves, each at many speeds, so every row is a unique pattern. Hover or tap a row.

Darker = closer to +1, lighter = closer to −1. Shown with 64 numbers per position instead of 512 so the pattern is visible, and laid out as in the article's figure. The paper interleaves the sine and cosine columns instead; the information is the same.

Because the pattern comes from a formula, the model can produce positions for sentences longer than any it saw in training.

3

Self-attention, step by step

Self-attention lets each word gather clues from the other words in the same sentence. It works like a search. Each word makes three new vectors from its embedding:

Query: what this word is looking for.
Key: the label each word advertises, so others can find it.
Value: the information a word hands over once it's found.

They're made by multiplying the embedding by three weight matrices, WQ, WK and WV, that the model learns in training. They have 64 numbers each, smaller than the 512 in the embedding.

The scores 112 and 96 are the article's example numbers for "Thinking"; the ones for "Machines" are made up the same way. Values are drawn as 3 cells instead of 64.

In a real model all of this runs as one matrix calculation for every word at once: Z = softmax(Q × KT ÷ √dk) × V. Here Q, K and V hold every word's query, key and value, and dk is 64, the length of a key.

4

Many heads are better than one

One round of attention gives one way of looking. The paper runs eight in parallel, called attention heads. This is multi-head attention. Each head has its own WQ, WK and WV, so each one learns to notice something different. The article calls these different representation subspaces.

Eight heads reading the same sentence

Each grid is one head. A row is the word being read, and the columns are the words it looks at. Darker means more attention, and each row adds up to 100%. Pick a head, then hover or tap a square.

Hand-drawn patterns of the kinds researchers find in trained models. The first two match the hero at the top of the page.

Eight answers become one

The next layer expects one vector per word, not eight. So the eight results (Z1 to Z8) are joined end to end, which is called concatenating, then multiplied by one more learned matrix, WO, to squeeze them back to one.

5

Shortcuts and tidying up

Two small details wrap every sub-layer, in the encoder and the decoder.

A residual connection adds the sub-layer's input back onto its output. It's a shortcut: the layer only has to learn what to change, and the original word is never lost on the way up the stack.

Layer normalization then rescales each word's numbers into a steady range, so they don't drift too big or too small after many layers. The paper calls the pair "Add & Norm".

6

Writing the translation, one word at a time

The encoder reads "je suis étudiant" once. Its final output becomes a set of keys and values that every decoder layer can consult. The decoder then writes English one word per step, feeding each new word back in as input for the next step. It stops when it writes a special end-of-sentence word, <eos>.

The decoder has two kinds of attention. Masked self-attention looks only at English words already written: future positions are hidden by setting their scores to −∞ before the softmax, so they get 0%. Encoder-decoder attention takes its queries from the decoder and its keys and values from the encoder, so each step can look back at the French.

From numbers back to a word

The top decoder outputs a vector. A linear layer stretches it into one score per vocabulary word, a list called the logits. A real model knows tens of thousands of words, so the logits are that long. The softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%.

Taking the top word every time is greedy decoding. Beam search instead keeps the best few unfinished translations (two, if the beam size is 2), extends each one, and keeps whichever turns out better overall.

7

How it learns

An untrained model starts with random weights, so its guesses are random. Training shows it a French sentence with the right English answer, measures how wrong its guess was, and nudges every weight a little to be less wrong. The nudging is backpropagation, and the measure of wrongness is the loss function.

The right answer is written as a one-hot encoding: a list as long as the vocabulary, with 100% on the correct word and 0% everywhere else.

Cross-entropy loss here is −log(probability of the right word). It's 0 for a perfect guess. Real training averages it over millions of sentences.

Even after training, every word keeps a little probability. That's the softmax at work, and it helps training: a wrong word with 1% can still be nudged.
