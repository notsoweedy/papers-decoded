The math inside attention

The Transformer guide shows what attention does. This page shows how the numbers are computed and why each step is there. Every number below is calculated live from a three-word example you can poke at.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKT ÷ √dk ) V

This one line is the heart of the Transformer, from the paper Attention Is All You Need. Click any part to jump to it. By the end you'll know what every symbol does.

Why compute anything at all?

  • Computers only do arithmetic, so every word has to become a list of numbers.
  • The model needs to measure relevance: how much should "it" care about "animal"? A single number for "how related are these two lists" is exactly what a dot product gives.
  • The model has to learn its own rules. Every step is smooth arithmetic, so we can ask "if I nudge this number, does the answer get better?" and nudge millions of numbers at once. That's training.
1

Words are lists of numbers

A vector is just a list of numbers. Each word starts as a vector called its embedding. In the paper it has 512 numbers, which is its dimension.

To keep every multiplication visible, this page uses a tiny model: 3 words from the sentence "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", and 4 numbers per word. In a real model the numbers are learned and nobody gives them names. Here they have names so you can read them.

The embedding matrix X

Stack the word vectors as rows and you get a matrix: a grid of numbers. This one has 3 rows (words) and 4 columns (features), so its shape is 3 × 4.

X, shape 3 × 4

"it" scores 1 on "pronoun": it needs to find what it refers to. Nothing yet tells it that the answer is "animal". Attention will.

2

The dot product: one number for "how related?"

The dot product of two vectors: multiply them number by number, then add it all up. For a = (a₁, a₂) and b = (b₁, b₂), it's a₁×b₁ + a₂×b₂.

Why this formula? It's big when the vectors point the same way, zero when they're at right angles, and negative when they point opposite ways. Geometrically, it's the length of a times the length of b's shadow on a. So it's a natural "similarity score".

Matrix multiplication: one recipe for every word at once

A weight matrix is a recipe for turning a vector into a new vector. Each column of W says how much of each input feature to mix into one output number. Applying it to a word is a set of dot products, one per column.

Multiplying the whole matrix X by W does this for every word in one go: matrix multiplication. The rule for shapes: (3 × 4) times (4 × 2) gives 3 × 2. The inner numbers must match, and the outer ones give the result's shape.

This is why GPUs matter: matrix multiplication is the one thing Tensor Cores do at full speed.

4

Why three different vectors: Q, K and V

Each word gets three vectors, made with three different recipes:

Query = x × WQ: what this word is looking for.
Key = x × WK: what this word offers, so others can find it.
Value = x × WV: what this word hands over once found.

Why not just compare the embeddings directly? Because looking for something and being that thing are different. "it" is a pronoun, but it's looking for a living thing. Its query can point somewhere its own embedding doesn't. And because the queries and keys use different recipes, "it" can care about "animal" a lot while "animal" barely cares about "it". Relationships in language are one-way like that.

The violet arrows are every word's key. The teal arrow is the chosen word's query. The score is their dot product: queries that point toward a key score high with it.

Row = word reading, column = word being looked at. KT is K transposed: flipped so its rows become columns, which lines every query up with every key in one matrix multiplication.

5

From scores to percentages: scaling and softmax

Raw scores can be any size and can be negative. We want attention weights: positive numbers that add up to 100%, so the result is a sensible blend. That's what softmax does:

weighti = escorei ÷ (escore1 + escore2 + …)

Why ex (the exponential)? It turns any number into a positive one, keeps the order (bigger score, bigger weight), and stretches gaps, so the best match stands out. And it's smooth, so it can be learned through. Dividing by the total makes everything add up to 100%.

Softmax, one column at a time

Reading:

Here dk = 2 (each key has 2 numbers), so we divide by √2 ≈ 1.41. In the paper dk = 64, so it's ÷ 8.

Why divide by √dk?

A dot product adds up dk products. If the numbers are random-ish, more terms means bigger totals: the typical size of a score grows like √dk. That's a statistics fact about adding up random numbers. Their spread, the standard deviation, grows with the square root of how many you add.

Big scores make softmax all-or-nothing: one word gets nearly 100% and the rest get nearly 0. Then training stalls, because the nudges (gradients) through those near-0 weights are tiny. Dividing by √dk brings scores back to a typical size of about 1, whatever dk is.

The weighted sum: borrowing meaning

Last step of the equation: multiply each word's value by its attention weight and add them up. The result z is the word's new vector, its own meaning blended with what it borrowed from the words it attended to.

As matrices: Z = A × V, where A holds every word's attention weights (3 × 3) and V holds every value (3 × 2). One more matrix multiplication.

That's the whole equation: scores (Q KT), scaled (÷ √dk), turned into weights (softmax), used to blend values (× V). The rest of the Transformer is about doing this well, many times over.

7

Multi-head: the same math, eight times, side by side

One softmax gives one blend, so one head tends to track one kind of relationship. The paper wants several at once (who "it" refers to, what's next to what, and so on), so it runs attention 8 times in parallel with 8 separate sets of WQ, WK, WV.

To keep the cost the same as one big head, each head works in a smaller space: 512 ÷ 8 = 64 numbers. Then the 8 results are concatenated (joined end to end) back to 512, and one more matrix, WO, mixes what the heads found.

8

Positional encoding: clocks with different speeds

Nothing in the attention equation knows word order. Shuffle the words and you get the same scores, just shuffled. So before the first layer, each word's embedding gets a position vector added to it:

PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos ÷ 100002i/d) and PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos ÷ 100002i/d)

Read it as clocks. Each pair of numbers (a sine and a cosine) is the tip of a clock hand. Hand i turns pos × ωi radians, where ωi = 1 ÷ 100002i/d is its speed. The first hand spins fast like a seconds hand; later ones crawl like hour hands. Together they give every position a unique reading, like a clock showing a unique time.

Move through the sentence

A small model with d = 8, so 4 hands. Drag the position. Then add an offset k to see where position pos + k lands (dashed).

Why this design? The values stay between −1 and 1, so they never swamp the embedding. Any length works, even longer than anything seen in training. And moving k words later turns every hand by a fixed angle, whatever pos is. That turn is a fixed matrix multiplication (a rotation), so a head can learn a rule like "look 2 words back" once and use it everywhere.

9

Add & Norm, and the feed-forward network

The residual: output = x + Sublayer(x)

Each sub-layer's input is added back to its output. Why: the layer only has to learn a correction, and during training the nudges flow straight back through the "+" without fading. That's what makes a stack of many layers trainable.

Layer norm: (x − mean) ÷ spread, then × γ + β

After each addition, every word's vector is recentred to mean 0 and rescaled to a spread (standard deviation) of 1. Then two learned numbers per feature, γ (scale) and β (shift), let the model choose the final range. Why: after many layers, numbers would otherwise drift huge or tiny, and the next layer would have to cope with any scale.

The feed-forward network: max(0, x W1 + b1) W2 + b2

Attention moves information between words. The feed-forward network then processes each word on its own: expand 512 numbers to 2,048 with W1, apply ReLU (keep positives, turn negatives into 0), then shrink back to 512 with W2. The b's are biases, small learned offsets.

Why the ReLU? Without it, two matrix multiplications in a row collapse into one, and the network could only learn straight-line relationships. The bend at zero is what lets it learn more complicated rules. About two thirds of a Transformer's weights live in these networks.

10

From vector to word, and how it learns

The top of the decoder gives a 512-number vector h. A linear layer multiplies it by a 512 × vocabulary matrix to get one score per word, the logits. Softmax turns them into probabilities, and the model picks a word.

In training we know the right word, so we measure how wrong we were with cross-entropy: loss = −log(probability of the right word). It's 0 when the model is certain and right, and huge when the right word got almost nothing.

Then comes the step that makes everything learnable. For softmax with cross-entropy, the gradient of the loss with respect to each logit is simply probability − target. It's positive for wrong words (push down) and negative for the right word (push up). Gradient descent nudges each number a little against its gradient.

Train the output layer by hand

The right answer is "thanks". Each step subtracts (probability − target) × learning rate from every logit. This is real gradient descent on the logits.

In a real model the same idea flows further back. The chain rule passes the nudges through the linear layer, the feed-forward networks, WO, every WV, WK, WQ and the embeddings themselves. This is backpropagation. Every number on this page would get its own small nudge, millions of times. That's how hand-picked weights like ours become learned ones.

Where the GPU comes in

Now the math connects back to Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr. Count the work in one attention layer (d = 512, 8 heads) for a sentence of n words.

Attention cost calculator

size of the score tables (all 8 heads, 16-bit numbers, one layer)

The weight multiplications (Q, K, V and WO: 8 × n × d² operations) grow in step with n. The attention part (Q KT and × V: 4 × n² × d) grows with n², so it takes over for long sentences.

Scaling, masking and softmax touch every number in those n × n tables but do almost no math on each. That's memory-bound work, the exact pattern from the Brrrr essay. FlashAttention fixes it with operator fusion: it computes attention in tiles inside fast memory, so the big table never goes to DRAM.
